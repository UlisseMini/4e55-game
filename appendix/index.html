1. The task: what “fires” means

  • Model. InceptionV1 (GoogLeNet), the original TensorFlow/Lucid release, loaded with lucent.modelzoo.inceptionv1(pretrained=True). Input convention: RGB pixels in 0–255 minus 117.
  • Images. ImageNet-1k from the Hugging Face dataset ILSVRC/imagenet-1k, all 50,000 validation and 1,281,167 training photos. Each photo is resized (bicubic) so its short side is 224 px, center-cropped to 224×224 and stored as JPEG quality 90. That crop is what the model sees and what the game shows.
  • The neuron's value on a photo. Channel 55 of layer mixed4e after the ReLU is a 14×14 map. Its value is the maximum over those 196 positions (its strongest response anywhere in the photo).
  • Normalization. Values are divided by the neuron's maximum over all ~1.33M photos, so 1.0 is the strongest photo in ImageNet.
  • Fires / silent. “Fires” means at least 30% of that maximum, which is the top 3.07% of training photos (the 96.9th percentile). “Silent” means under 5%, the bottom 16.1%. The 30% cutoff matches the essay's own definition of a strong response; the 5% cutoff is ours. Photos in between are left out.
  • Balanced sets. Every test set has equal numbers of firing and silent photos, so guessing scores 50%.
This only tests the two extremes. It is a much easier task than predicting how strongly the neuron responds to an ordinary photo.

2. How Claude predicts the neuron

Model claude-opus-5-5, effort low, max_tokens 4000. Each request contains one photo (the 224×224 crop), never any other photo, and never the photo's label. Claude answers with a probability 0–100 that the neuron fires; “says fires” means at least 50. Accuracy is the share of photos called correctly. AUC uses the probabilities as scores.

System prompt, with the description inserted:

You are testing how well a written description of a neuron lets someone predict it.
The neuron is a single unit inside an image-classification network. On each image it either fires strongly (ON) or barely fires (OFF).
Judge the image using only the description below. About half of all images are ON.

<description>
{description}
</description>

User turn: the photo, then What is the probability (0-100) that the neuron is ON for this image? Answer with only the number.

One answer across all description runs had no number. It is stored as null and counted as “silent”.

4. Result 2: searching for the best shape rule

To give the essay's idea its best shot, Claude searched for the strongest rule in its spirit.

  • Allowed words. Geometry (points, V's, tapering, convergence, angles, roundness, curvature, symmetry, orientation, scale) plus generic part words (face, head, ears, eyes, nose, snout, muzzle, front, tip, edge).
  • Blocked words, enforced by this check on every candidate: 
    \b(cats?|kitten|fox(es)?|dogs?|pupp(y|ies)|wol(f|ves)|cars?|vans?|trucks?|vehicles?|bus|train|tables?|animals?|mammals?|pets?|birds?|monkeys?|
breeds?|terriers?|spaniels?|pug|machines?|fur(ry)?|fluff(y)?|hair|glossy|shiny|sheen|gleam\w*|metal|paint\w*|orange|red|white|black|
colou?r\w*|textur\w*|soft|cute|creature)\b
  • Each round. Claude (Opus 5.5, adaptive thinking) saw the essay's claim, these constraints, the best candidates so far with their tune accuracy and error lists, and 24 labelled tune photos, mostly current errors. It proposed 6 new rules of at most 115 characters. Candidates were scored on the tune set with the judge in §2.
  • Two tracks. “Pointedness”: rules must stay about points, V's or tapering (18 candidates). “Geometry”: any geometric property (20 candidates, counting the starting sentences).
  • Selection. The top candidates were scored on the select set; the best of each track was scored once on val.

Paired tests on the same 1,000 photos (exact McNemar: a two-sided binomial test on the photos where exactly one prompt is right):

  • A vs B: 97 photos only A gets right, 32 only B; p ≈ 8.5×10⁻⁹.
  • A vs C: 41 vs 23; p = 0.033. A long irrelevant block costs about 2 points.
  • C vs B: 99 vs 52; p = 0.00016. The essay's content costs about 5 points on top of that.

Intervals in parentheses are 95% Wilson intervals on each accuracy alone; for comparing prompts on the same photos, the paired tests are the right ones.

What changes in Claude's reasoning

Shape words (regex \b(point|pointed|pointy|round|rounded|V-shape|V shape|wedge|angular), case-insensitive) appear in 100% of B's replies and 38% of A's. The two prompts disagree on 129 photos: on 97 the essay makes Claude wrong (68 of them firing photos it now calls silent, mostly round, flat-faced animals such as hamsters, Pekinese, Persian cats and otters), and on 32 it makes Claude right.

7.1 Pointed vs round with real proportions

400 random training photos (200 firing, 200 silent). Claude labelled each photo's main subject pointed, round or neither. Firing: 59 pointed, 88 round, 53 neither. Silent: 35 pointed, 99 round, 66 neither. Of the 281 pointed-or-round photos, “pointed fires, round doesn't” is right on 158 (56%).

7.3 A searched non-noun feature: “sleek sheen + an eye”

An earlier search over about 70 descriptions (gaze, mood, cuteness, texture, lighting, kiki/bouba and more) found two that predicted as well as nouns on 60 held-out photos: “sleek sheen + an eye” got 83% where the noun description got 85%. On a crop centred where the neuron responds, it got 97% (nouns 93%), but the crop does part of the work. On a harder set (photos whose class label predicts wrongly) it dropped to 57%.

Filtering real firing photos showed it was a correlate, not the cause. Compressing highlights kept 85% of the response for animals and 92% for cars; erasing the catchlights in eyes kept 99%. Slight blur (σ = 1 px) cut the neuron by 23% for animals while the model's confidence in the right class fell 6%.

7.4 The wiring

4e:55 = ReLU(bias + weighted sum of the 528 channels of mixed4d), through mixed4e's 1×1 branch (mixed4e has 832 channels: 1×1 branch 0–255, 3×3 256–575, 5×5 576–703, pool 704–831).

7.7 The game

The game uses 31,879 photos chosen to cover every percentile band of the neuron's response, which over-represents the rare strong photos. Each round flips a fair coin for ON or OFF, then draws a photo from that side with weight = (how common its 0.5-percentile band is in ImageNet) / (how common it is in the pool), so draws match ImageNet's real distribution within each side, like the Claude test sets. Default cutoffs: ON ≥ the 96.9th percentile and OFF ≤ the 16.1st, which select the same photos as ≥30% and <5% of the max (apart from 9 photos at the boundary). Percentiles are over all 1.28M training photos. The heatmap is the 14×14 response map, brighter where the neuron responds.

8. Caveats

  • The judge is Claude (one model), not people.
  • Only the extremes are tested (top 3% vs bottom 16%), which is far easier than predicting graded responses on ordinary photos.
  • The 30% cutoff follows the essay's definition of a strong response; the 5% cutoff is ours.
  • Test photos are new, but most firing val photos come from classes also seen during tuning.
  • Result 1 and 2 numbers use 200 photos each (about ±6–7 points). The essay comparison uses 1,000.
  • One neuron, one network.
